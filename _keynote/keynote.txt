## Part 1. Personal anecdote—when you first saw the power of Opening Doors for others
In late April of 2014, I was 27 years old, standing at the front of a lecture hall, just down the street.
It was room 525 in the brand new Min Kao building. I was proctoring the final exam for Dr. Mike Berry's spring CS 102 class. This was my last final as a teaching assistant. There were about a hundred and twenty students, heads down, working through the written part of their computer science final. This was their last gate before summer break, and you could feel the freedom boxed into the lecture hall, yearning to be set loose.
Like any college class, some of the students were deeply passionate about the subject. This was what they loved. Others were simply checking a “general education” box in order to graduate. They thought computer science would be easy or interesting enough compared to their other choices like stats or calculus.
===
One of those students was Micah. Micah was just shy of 6 feet tall, dark haired. Kinda generic college student from central casting for your “average student.” In the beginning, he mostly attended classes, got middle of the pack grades, and usually turned in his homework. He reminds me of so many of my colleagues and employees: they show up, do the work, and don’t make waves in either direction.
Micah never really stood out. But he was a curious student. As the semester went on, you could see him getting more and more energized by the subject. The “mostly showed up” turned into “always showed up.” I could see that something was clicking for him. Something was energizing him. I get energized just thinking about how excited he was—I can still see his face, his smile when he figured out “for loops” in the lab by printing out each iteration’s data.
That was when Micah saw the compounding benefits of automation.
So there I was, standing in room 525, in front of Dr. Berry’s CS 102 final exam. Students are finishing, turning in their blue books—remember those? Well, they’re probably coming back.
Then, Micah puts his pencil down, packs up, walks to the front, and instead of placing his blue book on the desk, he hands it to me. Then he proceeds to give me the biggest bear hug of my life, lifts me clean off the ground.
He puts me down, thanks me and tells me that he took this class just wanting a box checked, but somewhere in the semester he found a new passion for solving invisible math puzzles. Which is pretty much what computer science in industry actually feels like…if you add in solving those puzzles while driving a speeding Ferrari down a moving highway of burning cars and collapsing bridges, that’s exactly what it feels like today, but I’m skipping ahead.
Micah walked out of the room, and Dr. Berry turned to me and said, "I've never seen anything like that. The way you share your curiosity and your passion for computing, you've lit that kid on fire." Micah later told Dr. Berry that he was switching his major to computer science, and he wanted to thank me for showing him his path.
And that is why I'm standing here today.
===
I thought I had spent the semester teaching computer science. What I had really done was open a door. Micah was the one who walked through it.
But first, somebody had to make the door visible.
Good morning and Welcome to the inaugural AI-NO-vation Summit. My name is Denise Gosnell.
## Part 2. Who I am / Introduction of the “Duality of AI”
I love a good puzzle. Whether it’s the “invisible math puzzles” that Micah discovered he loved, or the practical business puzzle, or the 2000 piece jigsaw puzzle of the Appalachian Trail…I can lose myself in them for hours or days on end. Puzzles have never once felt like work.
I’m fortunate in that way. I love what I get to do.
===
After finishing my PhD, there was only one destination for me: Silicon Valley. I wanted to learn and build from the best. I wanted to learn how the best builders in the world turned code into products. But I also wanted to see how those products became things people could actually use. How people’s jobs or lives were impacted and enhanced by things that started out as an idea and some code.
While in Silicon Valley, I quickly fell into the gravity well of open source. Think of open source like academia at its best. Everyone's invited, everyone shares, and the reward isn't hoarding what you know. It's being the one who figured it out first and told everybody.
I became a leader in open-source graph technology and helped dozens of organizations like Netflix, Starbucks, Home Depot, FedEx, SnapChat, and the Federal Government connect data in ways that break silos. Of course, a broken silo itself is a duality—it breaks somebody's closed, comfortable model of how the world was supposed to work and replaces it with an opportunity for someone else.
===
Like that time - Right after my PhD at a healthcare startup here in Charleston South Carolina, I helped to expose a $20M medicare fraud scheme simply by connecting data sets together.
That same start-up built automated healthcare claims processing and saved people real money. But it did it by making pharmaceutical drug prices transparent and exposed games the PBMs were running… so you can guess what happened next: [pause] a PBM sued us out of existence.
After enough times, you stop seeing innovation as one thing. What you build as a door for one person is a wall coming down for someone else.
===
Here’s the important thing about that:
The technology doesn't pick. People do.
So here's something else about me. I've always felt like I lived in a duality between what makes sense to me and what everybody else says is true. This isn’t like colorblindness where I think green looks orange. Or my firm belief that Peyton Manning is the actual mayor of Knoxville, period.
It’s more that my biggest moments of synthesis often don’t arrive in the moment, while reading at my desk, or listening to a lecture back here in my grad days. Rather, they arrive later, when I’m moving.
Some of my best thoughts arrive while I’m swimming laps, for example. That is how my mind works. I understand by moving. Talking. Testing. Making.
===
If you go down the research rabbit hole on this, professionals in this space sort people’s creating and learning styles into four buckets.
1. Visual.
2. Auditory.
3. Reading and writing.
4. Kinesthetic.
I’m the kinesthetic type. Things click for me when I’m moving.
Traditional software development is really tailored for the reading and writing learners who can express ideas in languages like Python, Java, C++. If that’s hard for you, you were standing outside the door.
===
AI, however, is opening that door.
Someone who can describe the problem in plain English—or French or Mandarin or Hungarian—can begin to build their solution.
Someone who struggles to turn an idea into a polished memo can literally talk it through and discuss it with Claude to find the shape of their argument.
Someone overwhelmed by a dozen competing responsibilities can create a system that gives them more of their day—and more of themselves—back.
That is real. I see it happening in front of me. We all do.
When we open the door and show people through, we create a whole new group of builders who can finally be seen, and who build better human experiences.
===
Take J, my chief of staff. J is a former pro golfer. She won on the LPGA tour. Then, she excelled in a career as an operations expert in tech. I showed her how to use plain language to design an app in Lovable. She's built a whole series of apps since.
The one she is most proud of solved a problem that had her completely buried. She was running a charity golf event for Folds of Honor.
===
J built a Lovable app to manage the donations coming in and send status updates back out. It took her a few hours. Then she added live scoring. Then she added a way for donors to send messages to the players while they were out on the course. All of this in a weekend.
I'll never forget how excited she was to tell me her app got over a thousand views on the day of the event.
===
Or take an executive I work with, Karen. She owns tech for a multi-billion-dollar company. Before we ever met, Karen spent millions out of her own P&L on a project with Salesforce to modernize and secure her data infrastructure. A year and a half later, it still didn't work.
Karen walked into our first meeting like someone on a losing streak, bracing for one more. She'd been buried under a run of projects that didn't land.
===
Together, using open source and AI, we built. Ninety days later she had the thing Salesforce couldn't deliver in eighteen months, and it cost her about 40% less.
For J and Karen - I didn’t perform a miracle. All I did was open the door for them.
===
But even as I talk about the positives, we need to acknowledge that AI is not immune from the duality of technology. The disruption, the displacement, the fear.
It is not irrational for people to worry about their job.
It is not irrational for people to worry about their elderly parents falling victim to deepfake scams.
It is not irrational for people to worry about their children growing up in a less stable world disrupted more quickly than a UT freshman changes majors.
===
This is the Duality of AI.
AI can open doors, but just as easily, AI can close them.
AI can give voice to people who have been talked over—and it can make it easier to counterfeit someone’s voice and steal their identity.
AI can help a first-time builder create something valuable—and it can allow a small group of companies to control the tools on which every builder depends.
AI can give us time back which we can use for our families, our friends, or ourselves—and it can tempt us to automate away the relationships that make the time worth having.
Both sides are real. Neither is guaranteed.
===
AI is not Field of Dreams. If we build it, the positive side will not magically come. As much as I love James Earl Jones and want this to be true.
No. The positive side of the duality has to be built on purpose. It requires people in positions of leadership—in technology, in business, in society—to become door openers.
So that’s what I am really here to talk about today.
Doors.
I want to talk about three places where the Duality of AI is present in all of our lives. Where we already have the choice to open or close doors for…
…For individuals and their jobs.
…For communities and their prosperity.
…For our own agency.
===
I call these the Three Great Tensions of AI.
Tension 1: AI will create new jobs…and eliminate others without offering a path forward for individuals
Tension 2: AI will create shared prosperity…or extract it from communities
Tension 3: Ai will democratize control… or concentrate it in the hands of a few individuals and companies
Let me ask you: Who here wants to open doors for individuals, for their communities, and for society? [Raise hand — ask people to raise their hands if it isn’t obviously getting hands to raise]
Who here wants to close doors instead? [Lower hand]
Great. You’re in the right room.
But the “door closed” future doesn’t actually require any of us to hold ill will or purposefully close doors. It can emerge from thousands of smaller choices made in the name of speed, efficiency, convenience, growth. None of those things are bad. But they don’t have to lead to closed doors either.
That is the Duality of AI.
===
But let me say it again, the technology doesn't pick. People do. We Do.
So let me walk you through this Duality in the form of the Three Great Tensions of AI as I see them, and let’s see how we can all be door openers.
## The First Great Tension: AI Will Create new Jobs—and Eliminate others
Let me show you a quote I read recently that rings true to me:
===
[Read Quote] "Today we stand on the threshold of a new industrial revolution, the revolution of automation. This is a revolution bright with the hope of a new prosperity for labor and a new abundance for America. But it is also a revolution which carries the dark menace of industrial dislocation, increasing unemployment, and deepening poverty."
Anyone know who that was? Any guesses?
That's John F. Kennedy. Read it again and try to tell me it wasn't written for this moment. What does that tell us? What does it tell us that a quote from the early 60s is equally applicable today? I suspect if we looked hard enough—or asked AI to look for us—we could find something similar from the 1890s or 1920s.
===
In prehistoric times, Thog probably lamented the downfall of his sledge business when Grog invented the wheel.
We invent new things. Those new things bring change. Change is disruptive. That disruption has positive effects and negative effects. This is the inherent tension in change.
===
But humans don’t like change.
Macro, we are very adaptable as a species.
Micro, we individuals like our things the way we like them.
We may understand intellectually that change is inevitable. But when it happens to us—when it alters our routines, threatens something we worked hard to build, or removes a path we thought we could count on—it does not feel like progress.
It feels like loss.
And sometimes, this affects us in bigger ways, like our careers, paths we were counting on or built over years, decades, or even a whole lifetime.
That’s not just a loss of convenience or routine.
That’s a loss of identity.
We lose who we are.
===
What do we do then?
And that is important, because it is tempting for those of us who are excited about AI to dismiss resistance as ignorance.
We tell people they need to understand the technology better.
We tell them disruption has happened before.
We tell them that, in the long run, innovation creates more prosperity.
And that’s true, but tell that to the person who lost their job.
As with every major technological disruption, AI will eliminate jobs.
But equally inevitable is the creation of new work.
Sometimes, the skills transfer.
If you can make a sledge, probably you can make a cart. Perhaps you can learn to make a wheel.
But sometimes, the skills do not transfer easily.
When a robot automates an assembly-line job, it is technically possible that the displaced worker will retrain in advanced robotics or software engineering.
But “technically possible” is doing a lot of work there.
===
The evidence from previous waves of automation does not give us great confidence that everyone who loses a job will simply move into one of the new jobs being created. In 1950, almost 1.5 million people worked as telephone switchboard operators, either for the Bell system or in offices, hotels, and factories. By 1984 that number fell to 40,000. Today, it’s under 2,000.
These jobs disappeared even as new ones were created—first, people to design and build the automation systems, engineering jobs; then, people to maintain that automation, like linemen and other electrical and mechanical repair jobs; then, eventually, it’s computerized, which is a whole new category of jobs, and so on.
Those new jobs required different skills. Someone had to learn them, and someone had to pay for that.
They were in different places. Someone had to move, or be left behind.
They came years later. Someone had to live through the gap.
And many of them went to entirely different people.
On a chart, that looks like a recovery. To the operator who lost her job, it was not.
That is the part we risk losing when we talk only in aggregate.
===
We risk losing the part that makes us human.
## The Second Great Tension: AI Can Create Shared Prosperity—or Become Extractive
The second great tension is about who benefits—and who pays. It is the tension between innovation and community—whether innovation adds to community or extracts from it.
AI has the potential to create extraordinary value. It can help businesses grow, enable entrepreneurs to compete, and accelerate research. It can also be used to improve public services and bring entirely new investment or products to places and communities that stand to benefit.
But economic growth does not automagically equate to community prosperity.
===
A company can become more innovative and productive while employing fewer people locally.
Small businesses can gain powerful new capabilities and simultaneously find themselves competing against companies with vastly greater access to data, talent, and computing power.
And the physical infrastructure that powers AI can bring many millions or even billions in new investment while consuming the land, electricity, water, and public capacity of the communities where it is built.
The innovation may be global. The benefits may flow to customers, investors, and businesses around the world.
But the burden is local.
It is the community that supplies the land, the power, the water, and the public infrastructure. And there is no guarantee that the value created will return to the people who live there.
===
For many people, a data center is the first part of the AI economy they can actually see. Data centers are the pinata in the room they can hit.
An AI model is abstract. The algorithm is invisible. But the building at the edge of town? That’s concrete.
A data center can expand the tax base, support construction, strengthen infrastructure, and connect a region to one of the most important technological transformations of our time.
It can also draw enormous amounts of electricity, occupy land, use water, create noise, and require significant public investment.
The answer is not to stop building.
===
It is to build in a way that earns and sustains community support and trust. That means listening before the major decisions have already been made. It means being candid about costs as well as benefits. It means investing in the infrastructure a project requires rather than quietly shifting those burdens onto everyone else.
And it means asking what else we can build.
===
Dr. Katie Schuman and I were doing our PhD research at the University of Tennessee at the same time. I left for Silicon Valley. Dr. Schuman stayed and became one of the leading neuromorphic-computing researchers in the world.
Neuromorphic computing uses chips designed more like a brain than a conventional processor. For our purposes today, the essential point is power: certain learning tasks can be performed using a fraction of the energy required by standard approaches.
===
I toured Dr. Schuman’s lab. Her summer research students demonstrated chips learning the classic pole-balancing problem. Using less than 10% of the energy required using today’s GPUs. I watched a different computing future take shape on a sliver of the power—right here in Tennessee.
Then they handed me a tray of the early chips.
And I dropped them.
My stomach fell through the floor. I thought I had just dropped millions of dollars of research.
The students looked at me and said, “Oh, don’t worry. Those are from years ago. We’re already generations past those. We wouldn’t hand you one of the new ones.”
Which made me feel better and worse at exactly the same time.
But those chips represented something important.
===
We sometimes talk about the demands created by AI as if they are simply the unavoidable price of progress.
They are not.
They are shaped by decisions: what we fund, what we build, which technical approaches we pursue, and whose needs we consider along the way.
We can invest in more efficient architectures. We can support local research. We can build smaller and more specialized models. We can prepare local workers and businesses to participate. We can ensure that new infrastructure strengthens the systems communities will continue relying on long after construction is complete.
The leadership responsibility inside this tension is not to choose between innovation and community.
It is to connect them.
A community can gain investment and still feel strained by it. That is the tension. And we need to be honest about it.
Will AI help more communities build durable prosperity? Or will its greatest benefits pass through them—or pass them by?
Those questions are not anti-business or anti-technology. They are human business questions.
That is what we miss when we measure the opportunity of innovation and not the cost of what stays behind.
===
We risk hollowing out the communities we are supposed to be building up.
## The Third Great Tension: AI Can Democratize Control—or Concentrate It
And that brings us to the third great tension: control.
Who gets to build with AI? Who gets to inspect it, question it, adapt it, and decide how it is used? And who retains agency over the parts of their lives that AI increasingly touches?
This tension can be narrow.
===
[ZUCK QUOTE IN BACKGROUND ON SLIDE; DO NOT READ ALOUD: “You’re going to get a handful of companies that run these closed models that are going to be in control of the APIs and therefore able to tell you what you can build.”]
Will AI democratize software development, allowing far more people to build tools and solve problems more easily and more effectively than before?
Or will the essential technology be controlled by a handful of companies, with everyone else renting access on terms they did not set, having to forfeit their data, their privacy, their deeply personal thoughts?
But the tension can also be much wider.
===
[ALTMAN QUOTE IN BACKGROUND ON SLIDE; DO NOT READ ALOUD: “We see a future where intelligence is a utility, like electricity or water, and people buy it from us on a meter."]
Will AI give individuals more control over their time, knowledge, work, and choices? In short, will AI give people more control over their own lives?
Or will it give institutions more power to monitor people, predict them, influence them, and make decisions about them?
Believe me, I’m not an alarmist, but these kinds of quote by those responsible for building the AI tools that will power our future should give most sane people pause.
Is this future opening doors or closing them?
Here’s the part where I get to talk about what really gets me excited: Open Source.
===
The architecture of a technology helps determine the distribution of power around it. I have spent a significant part of my career in open source technology.
Open source does not mean that everything must be free, nor does it mean unsafe and insecure and that everyone has access to your technology or data.
Open source is about transparency and collaboration. It’s about security and innovation in a sort of “strength in numbers” sort of way. Because more people with different backgrounds and different needs are all relying on and improving on the same underlying code, and because everyone has a shared commitment to transparency and collaboration, everyone can benefit.
It’s a positive sum game.
On the flip side, closed is not a synonym for “bad.”
Closed systems can offer real benefits: integration, consistency, technical support, simplicity, and clearer lines of accountability.
Anyone who is an Apple user knows what I’m talking about. Apple controls the entire experience, and much of the time, that is precisely why the experience works so well. It’s a beautiful walled garden on the inside. If you’re into that sorta thing.
The risk calculus changes, however, when the closed system is not simply operating our phone or laptop, but determining things like what information reaches us, what choices are place before us, and what decisions are made for us.
Closed systems become problematic when they control too much for too many. Google often got in trouble in the EU for what was effectively a monopoly on the distribution of (and therefore access to) information.
AI is that, but on steroids.
===
We ask AI in chat what is true.
We ask AI what matters.
We ask it to summarize arguments, evaluate choices, recommend actions, organize our work, draft our words.
And increasingly, we ask AI to act on our behalf.
That can be extraordinarily empowering. Remember J’s charity golf app she built on Loveable?
Used well, AI can increase our capacity to think and act to reach beyond the limitations of our learned skills and unlock the potential of our imagination and creativity.
But the same technology can also weaken the very capabilities it appears to expand.
If we routinely ask AI what to think before we have tried to think, we risk outsourcing more than a task. We risk outsourcing the struggle through which judgment develops.
===
I’m talking about analytical cognition, about critical thinking.
Analytical cognition is not simply the production of an answer.
It is the process of examining evidence, tolerating uncertainty, recognizing assumptions, acknowledging our own bias, reconciling the evidence with what we know or thought we knew and sometimes even changing our minds.
AI is a tool that can enhance that process.
Or it can short-circuit the process if we just take the answer as gospel without putting in the critical thinking.
Don’t get me wrong. It’s convenient. So is Google. When’s the last time anyone went to a library or got into an argument at a bar with a friend over who had more passing Touchdowns, Manning or Brady?
===
But the convenience of AI can conceal a transfer of control. That’s the risk.
If a system chooses which sources to summarize, which arguments to emphasize, and which options to present, then it is influencing how we understand and act in the world.
That does not mean we should reject AI assistance, much less AI in general. It means we need to understand this tension clearly.
Are we asking AI to help us reason or asking it to reason for us?
Are we using it to expand our choices or allowing it to narrow them without our awareness?
Are we gaining greater control over our lives or becoming more dependent on systems we cannot inspect, challenge, or leave?
Open source is one response to that tension. It is not the only response, but it is oneI happen to love and one we touch every day. Because open source and openness in general is not just about access to code or innovation or collaboration. It is about control and freedom of choice. It is about whether we or anyone else outside the center retains the power to understand the system, to question its choices, to build upon it and if we so choose, it’s about the power to create an alternative.
Closed systems can also serve us well. But when a handful of companies control the systems through which we increasingly learn, decide, create, and act, something more fundamental is at stake.
That is what we miss when we measure everything AI can do for us without quantifying how much control we surrender in return.
===
We risk surrendering our agency to systems we cannot question, change, or leave.
## PART 4. STRATEGY AND EXECUTION: WRITING IS THINKING
Now, you all are leaders. In your community, at your company, in your industry. And like it or not, the hand we have been dealt is a life during a period of great change.
Change is hard. Successfully navigating that change requires good leadership.
Good leadership requires clarity—of strategy and of execution.
For my money, there is no better company at executing software strategy than Amazon. Amazon has sixteen leadership principles. You don’t need sixteen. You just need to know the underlying habit beneath all of them.
===
Writing is thinking.
Good strategic thinking starts at the end. What is the outcome we are trying to achieve?
At Amazon, we took that to the extreme. We called it Working Backwards. Before we built a product, we wrote the press release for the finished thing. Then we wrote our way back from that announcement to the very first requirements. The whole plan existed on paper before anything existed in code.
But here is the real trick. It was never about the documents themselves. It was about shared clarity. Finance, engineering, marketing, legal, operations, every function with its own needs and skills, all worked from the same small set of core pages.
One set of pages.
Many readers.
Everyone thinking together, everyone clear on what we were trying to accomplish.
===
Much like a football play. You may not be Peyton Manning. Maybe you’re Marvin Harrison. But you know not only where you are supposed to be, but you know where your other receivers are, where the blockers are, so if the ball comes to you, you know what to do after you catch it.
Same at Amazon. When it came time to execute, the strategy was clear to everyone, and we were all on the same literal page.
===
Back to the present. Here we are. In the world of AI, at risk of AI happening TO, at risk of the technology choosing instead of the people.
It is incumbent on us, the leaders in this room and other leaders in similar rooms around the US and the world, to exercise our agency and decide that it is not the technology that chooses, it is the people.
To get there, though, we need a strategy.
And that means we need to take a page from the Amazon playbook and get to a shared clarity.
But how do we do that? We lack clarity not because we haven’t done this before but because everything is new. It’s moving so fast. We can’t keep up.
Some of the most fundamental questions remain unanswered—what is AI going to do to us, to our company, to our society? What even is AI?
Well, I want to help you with that by offering a simple thought that will demystify this and bring some clarity:
===
AI agents are just files.
AI agents are just files.
Strip away the hype, and it’s just a set of instructions, some context, and the output you want back.
When you realize that, the Amazon playbook still works. Start at the end, write your way to a shared clarity of thought, and then execute. Yes, there is a major new variable to consider with the potential for great good and great bad. But it’s not magic.
If you take the time to write your strategy and think it through, you already have the inputs you need to take advantage of AI to the fullest. The thinking is the work. The agents just read.
Every leader in this room is deciding how their organization is going to navigate this moment, how to get more of the positive and less of the negative from the Duality of AI.
===
[Tension 1: AI will eliminate jobs and create new ones / Tension 2: AI will create shared prosperity or extract it / Tension 3: Ai will democratize control or concentrate it]
As you are thinking, and writing to think, I want you to have those 3 tensions in your mind.
The leaders in this room cannot make every consequence of AI positive. We cannot promise that no job will disappear, no community will face new costs, and no powerful institution will use AI in ways we wish it would not.
We cannot resolve all of these tensions.
But we can influence how the change is experienced.
We can help people move from one opportunity to the next. We can make the benefits more accessible. We can give more people a voice in the decisions that will shape their lives.
===
[RESOLUTION 1]
Some jobs will disappear. Some career paths will narrow. Some hard-earned skills will become less valuable. At the same time that AI is closing off some forms of work, it is dramatically lowering the threshold for creating others.
That is the leadership responsibility inside this tension.
===
Who in your organization could do more meaningful work if someone made these new capabilities accessible to them? Who has insight but not the title? Who understands the problem but lacks the technical vocabulary? Who has never been invited to see themselves as a builder?
So here is the first question I want you to carry out of this room.
===
Who gets the tools, and who gets a path when their old one closes?
===
[RESOLUTION 2]
AI investment can create enormous value. But the place where that value is created is not always the place where it stays.
That is the leadership responsibility inside this tension.
When you invest in a community, what capacity will remain after the announcement, the construction, or the initial wave of spending? Will local businesses be stronger? Will workers gain lasting skills? Will public infrastructure improve?
The goal is not simply to locate innovation in a community. It is to leave that community more capable than you found it.
That is not charity. It is stewardship. It is good business. It is creating durable economic growth and durable communities.
So here is the second question I want you to carry out of this room.
===
Who benefits, and who pays?
===
[RESOLUTION 3]
AI systems will increasingly help us understand information, make decisions, and act. It is a really powerful tool. Some of the systems that enable this will be open. Some will be closed. But in either case, the people who depend on them must retain meaningful agency.
That is the leadership responsibility inside this tension.
When you adopt an AI system, can your employees understand and question it? Can your customers control their data? Can your organization change providers if the technology, the terms, or the leadership changes? Are you using AI to strengthen people’s judgement or simply reducing the number of judgements they are allowed to make?
Openness does not mean open source. It means preserving the ability to understand, challenge, choose, and build something different. It means preserving agency, control.
So here is the third question I want you to carry out of this room:
===
Who can understand and question this, and who can leave?
===
Now, as you go forth and talk about AI with colleagues, gain knowledge, and write those documents I want you remember and ask yourselves those three questions.
And I want you to run that review on every single one of your AI decisions.
Call it the Open Door Test.
===
Before your next AI decision, whether it is a vendor contract, a reorg, or the first Amazon Style Working Backwards document you write on Monday morning, ask yourself: does this open a door for people, or does it just happen to them?
And then apply those 3 simple checks from our 3 tensions as part of the Open Door Test:
1. Opportunity. Who gets the tools and a new path, and whose paths close?
2. Prosperity. Who benefits, and who pays?
3. Control. Who can understand and question this, and who is forced out?
If you can answer all three, you are opening doors. If you cannot answer one of them, you have found the door that is quietly closing on someone.
## PART 5. CONCLUSION: BE A DOOR-OPENER
So let me tell you what is really lighting me up. Because it isn’t the chips. I’d still drop those anyway. It isn’t the layoff numbers, or the record number of new businesses opening, or the potential jobs waiting to be created. It isn’t even the inventions and new possibilities AI will unlock.
No.
I do what I do for that kid who sees an open door—a door I helped open—and walks through. That kid who hugs me, lifts me off the ground, and tells me she found a new way to see the world. That kid who discovers an entire set of possibilities on the other side of a door she did not realize existed before.
Because for her, it didn’t.
Until it did.
And all she needed was for someone to open the door and show her where it was.
That’s it. That’s the whole thing. I do what I do for the Micahs of the world. I would go so far as to say I exist to light somebody else’s fire—and to help keep it burning.
That is my philosophical North Star. It is what gives me energy. It is what gives me purpose. And every one of the dualities we just walked through comes back to that same purpose.
These dualities are different in scale—individuals, communities, society--but each presents us with the same choice:
Will AI happen TO people, Or will we help people have agency in what happens next?
The headlines want you to be afraid.
And that fear is not unfounded.
Change is hard.
Fast change is unsettling.
Fast change that affects individuals, communities, and society simultaneously, a mile wide and a mile deep, that is in a word, terrifying.
But fear has a way of narrowing our field of vision. It makes us look for safety in certainty. It pushes us to pick a side:
* acceleration or obstruction,
* optimism or pessimism,
* opportunity or danger.
The Duality of AI demands something harder from us.
It asks us to see both sides at once. To acknowledge the danger without surrendering the possibility. To pursue the possibility without pretending the danger is not real.
We do not have to choose between a future in which AI succeeds and one in which people do. It does not have to be zero-sum. But the better future does not happen automatically. It will take the people in this room—the leaders of businesses, institutions, and communities—to make it happen.
So that is my challenge to you.
===
Do not let a month go by when you cannot say, “I opened a door for someone.” Every month, find your Micah.
Find the person with the problem but not the title. The person with the idea but not the technical vocabulary. The person whose old path is disappearing. The person who does not look like you, think like you, learn like you, or arrive by the path you did.
You do not have to determine their future for them. You do not have to carry them through the door. Just open the door and make it visible.
Give them the tools. Help them reach the threshold. And then let them surprise you.
AI will open extraordinary doors.
AI will close some, too.
The technology alone will not decide which side of that Duality defines this era.
We will.
So keep it human.
And hold the door open.
